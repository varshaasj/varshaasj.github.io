How can a brokerage's staff get answers from their own documents without the AI inventing anything?

Before the hub, finding an answer meant searching for the right document by hand, then reading through it to work out the answer yourself. It was slow every time.

Timeline
September 2026 to now
My role
Team lead; product and UI; prompt and retrieval tuning; client demo
Team
Four, at Northwestern Forge. My teammates built the infrastructure that indexes the documents and runs the models.
Background
Advantage Futures is a futures brokerage. Its staff needed answers from internal documents.
Outcome
Demoed to the client and now being deployed on staff machines.

How we framed the need

We started from 21 user stories in five groups. These five shaped the interface most.

  1. Find it in a few wordsType a few words and get the right document, without knowing which folder it is in.
  2. Answers with sourcesEvery answer cites its source. If the documents do not cover the question, the hub says so instead of guessing.
  3. The exact sectionThe answer points to the section it came from, so staff can check it in context.
  4. When it was updatedEvery document shows when it was last updated.
  5. Current versions onlySearch and answers use only the latest version of each document.

Before building, we also wrote a press release for the finished hub and worked backwards from it.

Process

I tuned the prompts and retrieval settings until answers stayed inside the documents. Where the exact wording mattered, the hub quotes the document directly.

Why only the documents

Advantage Futures already had the answers its staff needed. The problem was finding them: a large number of documents spread across departments, and no quick way to know which one held the answer. So the hub works in two steps. It first finds the right document, and if that is not enough, staff can ask a follow-up question and the hub finds the answer inside it. At a regulated brokerage, the model should never fill gaps with assumptions, so it answers from the documents or says it does not know.

Building it

I built the whole interface and owned the product decisions: what a person sees, and especially what they see when the tool does not know the answer. My teammates built the infrastructure that indexes the documents and runs the models.

  1. First versionSearch and answers with citations
  2. v2I noticed answers felt slow, so now they stream in word by word.
  3. v3I caught "What is rule 3?" failing, so numbered sections are now quoted word for word.
  4. v4Long PDFs keep their real section names.

Outcome

We demoed the hub to the client, and it is now being deployed on staff machines. The client estimated it would cut the time staff spend finding information by 75%. The next step is a proposed move to Amazon Bedrock inside Advantage Futures' own AWS account, estimated to start answering in about a second.

What I took from it

Not every company should use all of a technology

On the surface, an internal AI tool is a question of capability. At Advantage Futures, it was a question of trust. The firm is highly regulated, and a data leak would cost it far more than it would cost most companies, so for its first internal AI application we kept its documents away from public models. The demo runs entirely on one machine the firm supplied, so no document text leaves it, and the next step we proposed moves the AI models to Amazon Bedrock inside the firm's own AWS account, reached over private network links, so the data still never leaves its control.

Building with Claude as a coding agent taught the same lesson from another side. The agent wrote code quickly, but the architecture and design decisions still had to come from us, built in pieces small enough to check. After two years of testing at UPMC and Zelis, I do not trust a system until it survives its edge cases. The "rule 3" failure surfaced only because I went looking for one.

What I would change next

Next, I would remove the filename from citations and show only the section and page. Marking up the screens made it clear that this is what people actually look for.

How can an analyst who is not a grid specialist see whether PJM will have enough power in 2030, and why?

PJM's grid serves 67 million people, and data centers are pushing up demand faster than new power plants can connect. Much of the new power will not arrive in time, and what does arrive counts for less than its size: a solar farm is credited with about 6% of its capacity at peak, a nuclear plant about 95%.

I simulated every plant waiting to join the grid 10,000 times, so the answer is a range of possible futures rather than one forecast. Then I built an app to show it.

I renamed the project so anyone could understand the question from the title alone. The redesign is for analysts who are not grid specialists, and the detailed analysis is still there in tabs underneath.

Building it

I worked alone, directing several Claude Code sessions. They wrote the code. I sourced the data, set the rules, and approved every change to the model before it went in. Every assumption is labeled as published, measured or chosen, so a reader can see which numbers are facts and which are my choices.

  • A read-only audit found that the "reform works" scenario counted the same effect twice. It was removed, and a second audit confirmed the fix.
  • I backtested the model from 2019 onward. Actual supply landed inside the predicted range near its low end, which suggests the 2030 shortfall may be understated.
  • Claude Code said Vistra's filing never mentions Meta, and I disagreed. Meta was on page 54, but its text extraction had missed it.

Outcome

In none of the 10,000 runs do new plants close the 2030/31 gap.

What I took from it

Let people reach their own conclusions

The model produced far more data than anyone could read, so the real design work was deciding what an analyst should see first. I thought through their day: which factors decide whether a project goes live, how prices and demand are moving, and where data centers are being built.

My first version told analysts what to conclude, in a dense paragraph. I realized they did not want my conclusion; they wanted the data and a way to test their own. The redesign replaced static charts with sliders, so an analyst can change an assumption and watch the answer move without touching the code.

What I would change next

With more time, I would rework the navigation. The assumptions sidebar stays visible on every chart, which worked well, but with ten metrics it becomes hard to know where to change what; separate cards or views may serve analysts better than one long page. Before choosing, I would sit with an analyst. I never had that chance during the hackathon, and that conversation is what would tell me which idea is right.

How do you study a market whose prices are hidden?

I was waiting for a resale concert ticket to drop in price. It went up instead: fewer than a hundred listings were left, and prices climb when supply is thin. I wanted to understand a market where the seller's stock is worth nothing the morning after.

Timeline
May 2026 to now
My role
Research and engineering
Team
Solo
Background
A resale ticket that went up in price instead of down
Outcome
A research project. It is not deployed and has no users. Written in Python with a C++17 port in progress.

Process

I built a ticket price collector, and the same day I learned that SeatGeek's public prices were closed. So I switched to prediction markets, which have the same shape: a hard expiry where every contract settles at zero or one, with free data I could check a simulation against. The contracts I used were about Rotten Tomatoes scores, Netflix rankings and Billboard debuts.

30 July

I mapped the order book's edge cases on paper before writing any code. I thought a buy at 100¢ against a sell at 95¢ would fill at 100¢. It fills at 95¢, because the order already waiting sets the price.

31 July

I recorded 478 real contracts every 30 seconds for six days, through 165 resolutions.

31 July – 6 August

I compared the simulation with the recorded market: a world where I can see everything against one where I can only see prices from the outside.

6 August

I found an error in the reference price that had flipped the measured effect. One random seed showed a 12× improvement, which turned out to be luck once I ran 50.

Mid August

I started the C++17 port.

21 September

Outcome

85% of a naive market maker's loss comes from the spread, not from the stock it holds. In the real data, spreads widen 21 times over when news arrives.

What I took from it

The data can reshape the question

Face Value taught me that a technology idea has layers. The first is the need: I love concerts, and I felt that buyers were not seeing honest ticket prices. The second is the data, and that is where the project hit a wall, because ticket sellers keep their prices hidden so competitors cannot match them.

That secrecy closely resembled how financial markets behave, and competing in IMC Prosperity had already drawn me to how markets set prices. Rather than force the original question, I let it evolve into one I could test with real, live prices: prediction markets, where every price is public and every contract settles at zero or one.

What I would change next

Next, I would make my simulated market maker widen its spread when prices move, as real ones do, and record more markets, so that six days of data is not the whole story.
